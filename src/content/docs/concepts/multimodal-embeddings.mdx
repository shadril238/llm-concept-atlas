## Definition and intuition

Multimodal embeddings map different input modalities into a compatible representation space so they can be compared. A shared dimension is necessary for a dot product, but learned alignment is what makes cross-modal similarity useful.

Read this lesson as a sequence: identify the input, follow the transformation, inspect the result, and explain a failure. The worked example and recorded lab below make those boundaries visible.

## How it works

### Two inputs, coordinated representations

The actual image encoder receives processed pixel tensors, while the text encoder receives token IDs and its own sequence convention. Their outputs are projected into a shared 512-dimensional space and normalized. A caption and image can then be compared by cosine. Arbitrary vectors of equal length from unrelated models do not have this property.

### Search in either direction

Text-to-image retrieval ranks image vectors for a text query. Image-to-text retrieval ranks captions for an image. The same similarity matrix can be inspected by row or column, but the interpretation of a softmax depends on its candidate set. The atlas uses actual OpenCLIP embeddings for three attributed photographs and descriptive text, with unrelated caption distractors retained.

### Similarity is a measured relationship

The frozen pretrained model encodes learned associations, not a guarantee about every visual detail. A correct top match among astronaut, cat, and coffee is a small controlled observation. Familiar sample photos may have appeared in pretraining; their use here is for inspectable mechanisms, not an independent accuracy benchmark. Raw scores and wording variants remain available.

## Worked example

The coffee photograph can be compared with a descriptive coffee caption, the bare word coffee, and unrelated airplane or car captions. Choose the recorded wording condition and inspect the actual score change. Remove one caption: the underlying cosine remains fixed while the displayed candidate-normalized softmax changes because its denominator changed.

{/* example:e6-multimodal-embeddings:start */}

```python
import json
from pathlib import Path
data = json.loads(Path('src/data/retrieval-results.json').read_text())
matrix = data['vision']['cosines']
for image,row in zip(data['vision']['images'],matrix):
    best=max(range(len(row)),key=lambda i:row[i])
    print(image['id'],'->',data['vision']['descriptions'][best],round(row[best],4))
print('shape:',data['vision']['embedding_shapes'])
print('These are actual model similarities, not manually assigned semantic vectors.')
```

## When to use it

Use aligned multimodal vectors for image search, text-guided collection browsing, candidate-label matching, and evidence discovery. Keep the model, processor, tokenizer, normalization, and candidate texts versioned together.

## Limits and common mistakes

Embedding similarity does not generate a caption or explain why a match occurred. Relative rankings can change with wording and available alternatives. Numeric proximity alone cannot certify object presence, counting, identity, or support for a generated claim.

## Check your understanding

<details>
<summary>Why can the two modalities be compared here?</summary>

The paired training and learned projections aligned their representations. Matching output widths alone would not be enough.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does removing a candidate change softmax but not cosine?</summary>

Softmax renormalizes over the remaining alternatives. The already computed vector dot product has not changed.

</details>
