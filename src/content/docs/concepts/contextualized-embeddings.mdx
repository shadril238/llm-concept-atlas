## Definition and intuition

A contextualized token embedding is a vector for one token occurrence after a model has combined its initial representation with permitted context and position information. The same vocabulary item can therefore receive different states in different sentences.

The word “bank” appears near “loan” in one sentence and near “flooded” in another. A static word row cannot itself distinguish those uses; contextual processing can change the occurrence-level representation.

## How it works

An input lookup starts from a vocabulary row. Each transformer block mixes information through attention and applies position-wise transformations, so a later hidden state depends on the available input and the model’s mask.

The layer and position are part of the representation’s identity. Comparing a first-layer state with a final-layer state, or averaging pieces without a policy, can mix different quantities. Record the tokenizer, word-to-piece mapping, selected layer, and pooling rule.

Contextual difference is not proof that a model has perfectly disambiguated meaning. A cosine comparison quantifies direction in a learned space; whether that space separates the distinctions your application needs must be evaluated. Raw contextual states are not automatically a high-quality sentence-retrieval embedding.

## When to use it

Use contextual states for token classification, span extraction, or inspecting how an encoder processes a specific input. Use a suitably trained sentence encoder when your application needs whole-sentence similarity. Keep an exact-token baseline when names/codes matter.

## Limits and common mistakes

A projected point moving on a chart does not reveal a model’s full reasoning. Truncating context can remove the disambiguating word. An uncased tokenizer may discard capitalization, and a multi-piece word needs an explicit aggregation or selection policy.
