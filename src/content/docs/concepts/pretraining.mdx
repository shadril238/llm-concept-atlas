## Definition and intuition

Pretraining fits a model’s parameters on a broad learning objective before a narrower task adaptation. In causal language modeling, it increases the likelihood of observed next tokens across a text corpus.

You cannot label every possible downstream task in advance. Pretraining supplies reusable statistical representations, after which adaptation or prompting can target a particular behavior. The choice of data and objective shapes what is reusable.

## How it works

For a causal sequence x1…xT, the objective sums negative log probabilities of xt given the earlier prefix. A lower training loss means the observed tokens became more likely under that objective. It does not directly measure truthfulness, fairness, or instruction-following quality.

BERT-style masked prediction instead selects hidden positions and predicts them using available bidirectional context. Tokenizer vocabulary learning is a separate process from fitting the language model’s embeddings and transformer weights.

Supervised task tuning uses task examples and labels or desired responses; preference tuning uses comparisons or rewards under a specified method. In-context examples change the prompt without optimizer updates. Keep corpus construction, validation selection, and a final evaluation suite separate to avoid measuring memorization of test cases.

### The important distinction

A model can memorize repeated text.

## Worked example

If the correct next token has probability 0.25, its negative log-likelihood is −ln(0.25) ≈ 1.386 nats. Raising that probability to 0.5 reduces the contribution to about 0.693. Neither number tells you whether a completed paragraph is medically or historically correct.

## When to use it

Pretraining from scratch is useful for research into objectives or for sufficiently resourced domains. For a learning project, inspect a small pretrained model and train tiny controlled tasks before attempting a broad corpus run. Adaptation is usually a more accessible starting point.

## Limits and common mistakes

A model can memorize repeated text. Evaluation data that appears in the training corpus can inflate apparent generalization. A published parameter count does not describe the corpus, filtering, compute, or full reproducibility of training.
