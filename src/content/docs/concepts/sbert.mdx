## Definition and intuition

Sentence-BERT (SBERT) is a shared-weight sentence-encoding approach that makes independently computed sentence vectors useful for comparison. Sentence Transformers is the broader library that implements many architectures and objectives; its models are not all literally BERT.

Follow the input, objective, update, and evaluation as separate steps. The worked example and experiment below connect the explanation to an inspectable implementation.

## How it works

### Shared weights, separate inference

A bi-encoder applies the same encoder/pooling parameters to each sentence. After training, each document vector can be cached and compared with a query vector. Sharing weights does not mean running two separate copies forever, and a fixed output width alone does not create semantic compatibility.

### Contrast with a cross-encoder

A cross-encoder processes the paired token sequence jointly, allowing token interactions across the pair before scoring. For all unordered pairs of 10,000 inputs, 49,995,000 pair evaluations are possible; independent encoding needs 10,000 encoder inputs plus vector comparisons. These counts are not hardware timings. Cross-encoder quality and bi-encoder efficiency depend on the actual task and model.

### Pooling and training are both part of the model

Masked mean pooling averages contextual states over retained tokens; CLS pooling uses a designated contextual token. Padding must not enter a mean. L15 starts from pretrained tiny BERT and trains a new sentence-level objective, not random language-model weights. Its 128-wide vectors, tokenizer, pooling config and trained encoder are saved together and reloaded.

## Worked example

The original pair classifier combines [u,v,|u−v|]. With u=[1,0.5] and v=[0.2,0.8], the six features are [1,0.5,0.2,0.8,0.8,0.3]. L15 uses actual 128-wide vectors, hence 384 classifier features. That training classifier is distinct from the deployed cosine-search interface; test both the objective and the intended vector use.

## When to use it

Use a bi-encoder for large searchable collections or reusable sentence features. Add a cross-encoder for a small candidate pool when joint comparison is worth its extra work.

## Limits and common mistakes

An arbitrary mean of pretrained BERT states is not automatically a trained sentence embedding. Similarity depends on pooling, normalization, truncation and the learned task. Our shared-weight CPU example does not reproduce the book’s full NLI/STSB training scale.

Author execution checks tensor/mask contracts, actual parameter updates, split provenance and artifact reload. It does not constitute independent lesson acceptance. Keep training quality, task quality, runtime and generalization claims separate.
